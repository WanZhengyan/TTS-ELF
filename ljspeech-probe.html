00 What LJSpeech can and cannot settle

Yes — but be precise about which risk it retires. The full proposal carries four independent risks, and LJSpeech reaches exactly two of them. Conflating the LJSpeech model with the paper model is the easiest way to waste the week that follows.

✓ LJSpeech settles this

  • Is the codec latent a workable flow target? Whether continuous CFM plus late discretization recovers correct codes at all.
  • Is latent_std right? The scale constant that breaks training silently when wrong.
  • Does average upsampling align? Single speaker makes this the easiest possible version of the alignment problem — if it fails here it fails everywhere.
  • Is the plumbing correct? Mask polarity, CE target indexing, restore_cond wiring.

✗ LJSpeech cannot touch this

  • Zero-shot voice cloning. One speaker means SIM-o is meaningless — there is no unseen voice to clone.
  • The comparison table. No standard split exists for LJSpeech; the published row needs LibriTTS.
  • Model capacity at scale. 3.46 M audio tokens cannot support 118.6 M parameters, so the LJSpeech run says nothing about the matched model.
  • Prosody diversity. A single reader's narration range is far narrower than LibriTTS.
Use ELF-XS (14.3 M), not ELF-B⁺ (118.6 M), on LJSpeech. 3.46 M audio tokens is 1 : 14,412 against ELF's own 50 B-token pretraining. The matched 118.6 M model will memorise the corpus and tell you nothing. The LJSpeech model is a diagnostic instrument, not a scaled-down version of the result.

01 Test 0 — the probe that needs no training

Run this first, today, before writing a line of model code. ELF's entire premise is that you can travel through continuous space and discretize only at t = 1. For audio that premise has a measurable precondition: the codebook cells must be large relative to the error your flow model will actually land with. If they are not, no amount of training fixes it.

Three things to measure, all CPU-cheap

  1. Codebook margin. For each real latent, d₁ = ‖z − c_nn1‖ and d₂ = ‖z − c_nn2‖. Histogram (d₂ − d₁)/d₁. The fraction below 0.1 is the fraction of frames that are already nearly ambiguous before any noise — if that fraction is large, the codebook is clustered and late discretization is fighting the geometry.
  2. Noise tolerance. The two curves above. Report σ at 95 % code survival for both paths, and the WER/UTMOS of the decoded waveform at each σ.
  3. latent_std and the ceiling. Mean and std of the latents, plus the plain encode→decode resynthesis WER/UTMOS on your LJSpeech test split. The second is the ceiling every later number is measured against.

Gate: passed. Stated in units of latent_std the budget looks tight at 8.7 %, but that is a unit artifact — a 512-d Gaussian's norm is √512 times its per-dimension σ. In the geometry that matters, the flow model may miss x₀ by 0.73× the distance the codec's own quantizer already moves the latent, and still land on the correct code 95 % of the time. That is a workable bar, and it is the right comparison because the codec's rounding error is the irreducible floor either way.

The margin distribution is the more interesting finding. A 90× spread between p5 and p95, with 51.5 % of codes surviving a perturbation 8.3× the cell radius, says errors will not be spread evenly — they concentrate in the ~13 % of frames sitting near a decision boundary. That sharpens the prediction for path B: nearest-neighbour rounding treats every frame alike, whereas a trained classifier can learn which codebook neighbours are confusable and lean on context to separate them. The fragile 13 % is exactly where ELF's CE head should win, and the size of that win is the measurement worth publishing.

Path B needs a trained CE head, so measure path A and the margins now, and fill in path B right after Test 2. The comparison is the deliverable; the order is just scheduling.

03 Test 2 configuration

Split follows StyleTTS 2, which is the most-cited LJSpeech division: 12,500 train / 100 val / 500 test. State it explicitly in any writeup — LJSpeech has no canonical split, so an unstated one is uncomparable.

04 LJSpeech-specific traps

TrapWhy it bitesWhat to do
22.05 kHz vs 24 kHz WavTokenizer-large-600 expects 24 kHz. Resampling up invents no information and the resampler's own artifacts lower your ceiling before the model sees anything. Resample once, offline, with a good filter. Measure the ceiling after resampling — that is the real bound, not the published WavTokenizer figure.
Batch size 512 ELF's default gives 15 steps per epoch on LJSpeech. The model sees the same 13,100 clips thousands of times with almost no gradient diversity. 32. And remember lr = blr × bs/256, so the effective learning rate moves with it — print the derived value at startup rather than trusting the override took.
Single speaker hides mask bugs With one voice, a broken prompt mask still produces plausible audio, because the "wrong" voice is the only voice. The bug then surfaces on LibriTTS as mysteriously weak cloning. Test 3 exists for this. Check pitch and pace continuity across frame P explicitly, not just intelligibility.
Character-level text LJSpeech transcripts carry spelled-out numerals and abbreviations inconsistently. A character model learns the inconsistency. Normalise once, offline, and keep the normaliser identical for LibriTTS so the two runs stay comparable.
Narration prosody One reader's audiobook range is narrow. A model that sounds good here can still collapse on LibriTTS's 2,456 speakers. Treat a Test 2 pass as "no blocking bug", never as evidence the method works at scale.
